01Critical corrections to the December docs

This guide works with the hardware you already have. Four things would cause damage, instability or a physical dead end on the first power-up. All four are fixed by how you assemble and wire it, not by buying different parts.

Test-fit the PiCap before you commit

The DIGISHUO kit’s case is built for a GPIO-mounted 3.5″ screen, and its cooling fan sits on a tower of heatsinks above the SoC. Both can physically collide with the PiCap, which needs the full 40-pin header clear and roughly 10–15 mm of clearance above the board. Before fitting anything permanently: dry-fit the PiCap onto the bare Pi with no case and no fan, check nothing binds, then decide what to leave off.

new · see Phase C

Power the Pi separately

The PiCap’s onboard regulator is rated 1.5 A. A Pi 4 needs a 3 A supply. For the proof of concept, use the official 5.1 V 3 A USB-C supply and leave the PWR PI jumper off. Never power the Pi from two sources at once.

was: hardware_setup.md §2.4 “PiCap has regulator”

Don’t run string power through the PiCap

The only fuse in the manual is a 2 A board fuse. Take DATA and GND from the PiCap port. Feed +12 V and 0 V to each string from a fused distribution block.

was: test-bench diagram, PSU → PiCap → pixels

The 350 W supply is marginal

At the 0.72 W/pixel datasheet figure, 500 pixels at full white draw 30 A. The supply is rated 29 A, and 23 A is the 80% continuous limit. One tap on “white, 100%” in Home Assistant draws that full load. Keep the supply you have: measure a real string in step F6 and set FPP’s brightness cap from the result. This doesn’t affect a 50-pixel bench test.

was: 0.05 A/pixel, “17% headroom”

04Power & data wiring

This is the installed layout. For the proof of concept, wire only F3 and one 7.5 A way: the official USB-C supply replaces the DC-DC converter, and a single test string on Port 1 replaces the zones. The DC-DC converter is an optional change for the install; a USB-C supply on a GPO inside the PSU enclosure also works. Every +12 V feed leaves through its own blade fuse. Every 0 V return lands on one bus bar, which is also the PiCap’s ground, so data and power share a reference.

Mains. The LRS-350-12 is an open-frame supply with screw terminals. Australian rules restrict unlicensed mains work, so have a licensed electrician terminate the AC lead, or buy a pre-wired, enclosed 12 V supply with a moulded plug. Everything downstream of the PSU is 12 V or 5 V and is yours to wire.

06Build steps

Do phases A to F on a bench indoors, with one test string, before anything goes up a ladder. Ticks are saved in this browser only. Menu paths match FPP 10.1; if a label differs slightly on your version, the setting will be in the same menu.

ACheck the kit you have

Bench

  1. Controller parts

    • Raspberry Pi 4, 8 GB (DIGISHUO kit) with its case, fan + heatsinks and 64 GB microSD — don’t pre-load anything on the card, it gets wiped in Phase B
    • Falcon PiCap with its jumpers, and a CR1225 coin cell for the RTC
    • Official 5.1 V / 3 A USB-C supply — check the box; it isn’t itemised in the DIGISHUO listing and is the one thing worth confirming before the bench session
    • Ethernet cable to the router, and a laptop on the same LAN

  2. Power parts

    • The 12 V PSU with its AC lead terminated by an electrician. If it is still bare screw terminals, stop here until that is done.
    • At least one inline blade fuse holder (3 A for the PiCap feed, 7.5 A for the test string). The full fuse block and 0 V bus bar from §02 can wait for the install.

  3. Test string and tools

    • One 50-pixel string and a matching 3-pin pigtail
    • Multimeter (required), a DC clamp meter if you have one, small flat screwdriver, wire strippers, ferrules

BFlash FPP to the SD card

Laptop · 15 min

  1. Download the Pi image

    From the FPP releases page, download the Raspberry Pi image for the latest stable release (10.1.2 at the time of writing). The file ends in -Pi.img.zip; if a 64-bit image is offered, take it. The BeagleBone images will not boot on a Pi.

    https://github.com/FalconChristmas/fpp/releases

  2. Write it with Raspberry Pi Imager

    Choose Device → Raspberry Pi 4 then Choose OS → Use custom and select the .img.zip directly; there is no need to unzip it. Choose the SD card and click Next. When asked about OS customisation, choose No. FPP is its own image, not Raspberry Pi OS, and does not apply those settings. balenaEtcher works as well.

  3. Let it verify, then eject

    Don’t skip verification. A bad write is the most common cause of first-boot failures.

CAssemble the Pi and PiCap

Bench · power off

  1. Strip the case back and dry-fit the PiCap

    Remove the case’s top layer/lid, the fan and the tower heatsink before doing anything else — they’re sized for a bare desktop Pi or a GPIO screen, not for stacking a HAT. Sit the PiCap on the header with no power connected and check: all 40 pins engage flat, the board sits level, and nothing on the Pi (fan shroud, tall heatsink, screen standoff posts) touches the underside of the PiCap. If the fan or a tower cooler is too tall, leave it off; low-profile heatsinks on the SoC and RAM chips alone are enough for FPP’s load inside a vented enclosure.

  2. Fit the RTC battery

    CR1225, positive side up. The RTC keeps schedules correct if the Pi boots without network time.

  3. Set the jumpers for 12 V

    • PWR SEL: two jumpers, on pins 1–2 and 3–4 (7–24 V input).
    • PWR PI: off. The Pi gets its own supply.

    The single-jumper position (pins 2–3) is for 5 V input. Feeding 12 V with that setting can destroy the PiCap and the Pi.

  4. Seat the PiCap for real

    Line up all 40 header pins before pressing down; an offset by one row is easy to miss. Fit standoffs between the Pi and the PiCap so the board can’t flex when you tighten the screw terminals — the case’s own standoffs are usually the wrong height for a HAT, so use PiCap-specific ones rather than reusing the case hardware.

  5. Insert the SD card and plug in Ethernet

    Leave the pixels and the 12 V supply disconnected for now. The software setup needs only the Pi.

DFirst boot and find it

Bench · 10 min

  1. Power the Pi from the USB-C supply

    The first boot expands the filesystem and may reboot once. Allow two to three minutes.

  2. Open the FPP web UI

    http://fpp.local

    If that doesn’t resolve, find the Pi’s IP address in your router’s device list. The 192.168.86.x subnet suggests Google/Nest Wifi, where the list is under Google Home → Wi-Fi → Devices. Or run this from the laptop:

    ping fpp.local
arp -a

  3. Confirm what you’re running

    The Help → About page shows the FPP version, platform (Raspberry Pi 4) and the eth0 MAC address. Write down the MAC for the DHCP reservation.

ESecure, name and pin the address

Bench · 15 min

  1. Change the default SSH password

    The default login is fpp / falcon, which is widely known.

    ssh fpp@fpp.local
passwd

  2. Set a web UI password

    The web UI has no password by default. Set one in Status/Control → FPP Settings (UI section) and store it in your password manager.

  3. Hostname, time zone, NTP

    In FPP Settings, set the hostname to fpp-house (or another name you prefer; it will appear in MQTT topics). Set the time zone to your Australian city and turn NTP on. After a reboot the UI is at http://fpp-house.local.

  4. Give it a fixed address

    Preferred: leave FPP on DHCP and create a reservation in the router for the MAC from D3. Alternative: Status/Control → Network → eth0 → Static, using an address outside the router’s DHCP pool. Record the address; Home Assistant and xLights will use it.

  5. Update and confirm Player mode

    On Help → About, apply any update offered, then reboot. On the status page, confirm the mode is Player, which is correct for a single controller. Remote mode is only for a unit slaved to another FPP.

FOutputs and first light

Bench · 50-pixel test string

  1. Set the PSU voltage before connecting anything

    With the PSU unloaded, measure +V to −V and adjust V-ADJ to 12.2 V. Then shut down the Pi and switch the PSU off.

  2. Wire the bench (a cut-down version of the diagram)

    • PSU +V → 3 A inline fuse → PiCap PWR IN +. PSU −V → PiCap PWR IN GND.
    • PSU +V → 7.5 A inline fuse → test string V+. PSU −V → test string GND.
    • PiCap Port 1 DATA and GND → the string’s data and ground. The port’s V+ pin is live at 12 V — cap or insulate it if your pigtail carries all three wires; don’t let it touch anything. The data arrow on the pixels points away from the PiCap.
    • Pi on its USB-C supply. No fuse block or bus bar is needed for one string.

  3. Pre-power checks with the meter

    • No continuity between +12 V and 0 V at the PiCap or the string.
    • Continuity from the PiCap GND to PSU −V (common ground).
    • PWR PI jumper still off. USB-C connected to the Pi.

  4. Enable the PiCap output

    Input/Output Setup → Channel Outputs → Pixel Strings, select PiCap and enable output. Set Port 1: 50 pixels, start channel 1, colour order RGB, brightness 100. Save, then restart FPPD when prompted.

    Enabling this output disables the Pi’s onboard analogue audio, because the two share the PWM hardware. That is expected. For music shows later, add a USB audio adapter.

  5. Test and fix colour order

    Status/Control → Display Testing: enable the test and run the RGB cycle. If “red” shows green, try GRB. Change the order until red, green and blue each show correctly, and write it down, because a different batch of pixels can differ.

  6. Measure real current

    Set the whole test string to white at 100% and clamp the +12 V feed. Enter that reading into “Measured: 50-px string” in §05 and click Use this reading — it works out watts per pixel for you and updates the brightness cap for the full install.

  7. Enter the production channel map

    Once the site survey fixes the real counts, update the ports. With 250 pixels per zone:

    PortZonePixelsStart chEnd chBrightness
    P1Zone 12501750from budget
    P2Zone 22507511500from budget

    Each pixel uses 3 channels. End channel = start + pixels × 3 − 1.

GZones as overlay models

FPP UI · 10 min

  1. Create one model per zone

    Input/Output Setup → Pixel Overlay Models, then add:

    Zone1   start 1    channels 750   Horizontal, 1 string, 1 strand
Zone2   start 751  channels 750   Horizontal, 1 string, 1 strand
House   start 1    channels 1500  Horizontal, 1 string, 1 strand

    Each model becomes one light in Home Assistant, so name them the way you want them to appear on the dashboard.

  2. Test each model in FPP

    Fill each model with a solid colour from the overlay models page and check that the right pixels light.

HHome Assistant: broker and MQTT

HA UI · 15 min

  1. Install the Mosquitto broker add-on

    Settings → Add-ons → Add-on Store → Mosquitto broker → Install. Turn on Start on boot and Watchdog, then Start. No MQTT integration exists on this instance yet, so this is new infrastructure.

  2. Create a user for FPP

    Settings → People → Users → Add user: username fpp with a strong password. Mosquitto authenticates against Home Assistant users. The names homeassistant and addon are reserved.

  3. Accept the MQTT integration

    Settings → Devices & services should show MQTT as discovered. Click Configure, then Submit. This lives in HA’s UI config, so nothing changes in the haconfiguration YAML.

  4. Reserve the Green’s IP address too

    FPP connects to the broker by IP, so the Home Assistant Green needs a DHCP reservation as well. Note the address.

IConnect FPP to Home Assistant

FPP UI + HA UI · 15 min

  1. Point FPP at the broker

    Status/Control → FPP Settings → MQTT: host = the Green’s IP, port 1883, username fpp and its password. Leave the topic prefix at its default. Save and restart FPPD.

  2. Watch the messages arrive

    In HA, Settings → Devices & services → MQTT → Configure, then listen to the topic below. FPP status messages should appear within a few seconds.

    falcon/#

  3. Install the Home Assistant plugin in FPP

    Content Setup → Plugins, then install Home Assistant MQTT Discovery (fpp-HomeAssistant, published by the FalconChristmas project). Restart FPPD.

  4. Choose what to expose

    Content Setup → Home Assistant: tick the Zone1, Zone2 and House overlay models. Save.

  5. Test from Home Assistant

    A new MQTT device appears with one RGB light per model. Test on/off, colour and brightness, then confirm the entity IDs in Developer tools → States before referencing them in automations, as the haconfiguration CLAUDE.md requires.

    Control is one-way: changes made inside FPP are not reported back to HA. If you later want playlist control, the HACS ha-fpp integration adds FPP as a media player.

JReady for the enclosure

Bench → site

  1. Install only: move the Pi off the bench supply (optional)

    Keeping the USB-C supply on a GPO inside the PSU enclosure is fine. If you would rather have a single mains entry, add a 12–5 V DC-DC converter such as the DDR-15G-5. Power it from F3 with the Pi disconnected, trim its output to 5.1 V, then connect the USB-C pigtail and remove the bench supply. Run the full-white test and check for undervoltage:

    vcgencmd get_throttled
# throttled=0x0 means no undervoltage since boot

  2. Back up the configuration

    Download a settings backup from FPP’s Backup page (Status/Control menu). Keep a second SD card flashed with the same FPP version so a card failure takes minutes to fix, not an evening.

  3. Record the as-built

    • Hostname, IP, MAC, FPP version, colour order, channel map, brightness cap
    • Fuse ratings and cable sizes as installed (fill in the cable schedule)
    • Passwords in the password manager, not in the repo

08Project review

Findings from a full pass over the home-lighting repo, the haconfiguration repo and current FPP documentation. The hardware is already on hand, so these are corrections to wiring, configuration and documentation rather than purchases. Items marked “Fix now” affect the proof of concept.

Fix now

Generic Pi 4 kit case/fan not built to stack a HAT

The DIGISHUO starter kit is built for a bare desktop Pi or a GPIO-mounted 3.5″ screen, not for a HAT sitting flush on the header. Its cooling tower can be tall enough to collide with the PiCap. Dry-fit before final assembly (Phase C step 1); low-profile heatsinks alone are enough for FPP in a vented enclosure.

DIGISHUO kit listing, this session
Fix now

Pi powered through the PiCap regulator

A 1.5 A regulator is below the Pi 4’s 3 A requirement. Expect undervoltage throttling and SD card corruption under load. Corrected in this guide; the note in hardware_setup.md now points here.

docs/current_state/hardware_setup.md §2.4
Fix now

Supply sized on 0.05 A per pixel

At the 60 mA datasheet figure, 500 pixels exceed the LRS-350-12’s rating. Home Assistant makes full white a one-tap action. Keep the supply you have: measure a real string, then cap FPP brightness from the result (about 75% at the datasheet figure).

Fix now

AC termination not assigned

The docs say “hire an electrician if unsure”. For an open-frame 240 V supply in Australia, make it a required step: an electrician terminates the lead, or buy a pre-wired supply.

Enclosure heat

At 300 W the LRS-350 sheds about 35 W as heat. A sealed IP65 box under cover will cook it in an Australian summer. Use a vented enclosure with filtered, downward-facing vents, and lower the brightness cap in summer.
